When a Lebanese business sells to clients in the UAE or Saudi Arabia, its website usually does the first meeting. Gulf buyers compare several suppliers before calling anyone, and they judge each one on what the site proves: specific services, finished work at a relevant scale, real people, and how quickly someone answers. A site that carries that evidence in English, quotes clearly and responds within Gulf working hours makes a Lebanese supplier feel as close as a local one. Our [web design for the UAE](/web-design-uae/) page covers how we build for that market.

## Why does the website matter more when selling abroad?

Because there's no other first impression. A client in Beirut might know someone who worked with you, pass your office or meet you at an event. A client in Dubai or Riyadh usually can't. They find you, or are sent your name, and then they look you up.

In that moment your website is answering the question every remote buyer has: is this company real, capable and reliable enough to trust with money from another country? Everything on the site either helps answer yes, or adds doubt.

## What evidence do Gulf clients look for?

Specifics. Gulf buyers are used to being pitched by many suppliers, and they discount claims that every supplier makes. What persuades them is evidence:

- **Named services,** described concretely: what you do, what's included, what isn't.
- **Finished work** at a scale that matches theirs, with what you did and what changed. Anonymised is fine if the client requires it.
- **A clear process:** how a project starts, who they'll deal with, how long things take.
- **Real people,** with names, roles and photos. A team page of stock images reads as a company with something to hide.
- **Credentials that can be checked:** registrations, certifications and memberships, named precisely.

If your best work is under a confidentiality agreement, describe it by sector, scope and result without naming the client. That's still evidence, and Gulf clients understand the reason.

## What language should the site be in?

**English first** for most business clients in the UAE and much of the Gulf. It's the working language of business there, and it's what most buyers will read your site in.

**Arabic** matters more for government-linked work, some sectors and many Saudi clients. If you add it, do it properly: every page translated by a fluent speaker, with a real right-to-left layout. A half-translated Arabic version does more harm than none. [Arabic, English or both?](/blog/bilingual-website-arabic-english-lebanon/) covers how to build a bilingual site.

## How should you show prices?

If you publish prices or ranges, US dollars work for most Gulf clients, since the dirham and riyal are both pegged to the dollar. Some clients prefer to see local currency for their own approvals, and showing both is simple.

Two details prevent the most common awkward conversations:

- **Say whether prices include VAT** or other taxes that apply to the client.
- **State the payment terms:** deposit, milestones, and how payment can be made.

Publishing a starting price or range also filters out mismatched enquiries before they waste anyone's time. Our own [prices are on the site](/faq/) for exactly that reason.

## Does speed still matter if the client has fast internet?

Yes, for a different reason. In Lebanon, a slow site loses visitors on weak connections. In the Gulf, connections are fast, so a slow site stands out as a sign of poor work, especially for a supplier selling anything digital. If your own site is slow, buyers assume theirs would be too.

A fast site is also what Google and AI assistants favour when someone in the Gulf searches for a supplier. [Why site speed decides rankings](/blog/website-speed-google-rankings/) explains the link.

## How fast should you reply?

Fast enough that distance stops being a factor. The Gulf working week and hours differ slightly from Lebanon's, but the time zones overlap almost completely, which is a real advantage over suppliers in Europe or Asia. Use it:

- **State a reply time** on the site, such as "within one business day", and keep it.
- **Offer WhatsApp,** answered during Gulf business hours. It's how much Gulf business communication actually happens.
- **Name a person** who handles enquiries, so the client knows who they're dealing with.

A supplier who replies within hours feels local. One who replies in three days feels foreign, whatever their website says.

## Do you need a local presence?

Not to start. Many Lebanese businesses serve Gulf clients entirely remotely, and a strong website plus fast replies goes a long way. As Gulf work grows, a local phone number, a registered entity or a local partner can help with larger clients and some contracts, and it's worth checking early what a specific client or sector requires.
