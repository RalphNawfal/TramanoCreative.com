Local search in Lebanon differs from the advice written for the US or UK in three ways that matter: your customers search for the same thing in three different scripts, their intent is neighbourhood-level rather than city-level, and the technical bar set by your competitors is lower. The first two are things you have to design for. The third is an advantage while it lasts.

## Why does script matter so much here?

Because the same customer types the same query three different ways depending on their keyboard.

Someone looking for manakish may type it in Arabic script, or as *mankouche*, or as *manaeesh*. Someone looking for a hairdresser in Achrafieh might type *coiffeur Achrafieh*, or the Arabic, or a transliteration of either. These are not different customers with different intents. They are the same person, and which spelling they use depends mostly on what their phone keyboard is set to.

Three practical consequences:

**Optimising for formal Arabic alone misses a large share of your own market.** A significant proportion of local searches arrive in Latin characters because switching keyboards is friction and people avoid it.

**Optimising for English alone misses the rest.** Particularly outside the more French- and English-leaning districts.

**Assistants handle this better than search engines do**, because they normalise across languages — but only if your site gives them something to normalise. [More on being readable to assistants](/blog/getting-cited-by-ai-assistants/).

The practical approach is not to write everything three times. It is to check what your existing customers actually type — look at how they message you — and to make sure the site uses that language naturally somewhere, rather than the version a keyword tool spells correctly.

## Why is intent neighbourhood-level rather than city-level?

Because Beirut is small enough to cross and congested enough that nobody wants to.

Nobody searches for a dentist in Lebanon. They rarely even search for one in Beirut. They search for one in Achrafieh, or near a landmark, or simply *near me* while standing in the street. Fifteen minutes of traffic is a real reason to choose a different supplier, so the search reflects it.

A site that never names the areas it serves is absent from most of the queries that would have found it. That is the gap — and it is why competitors build neighbourhood pages.

**Do it in the copy, not the footer.** Name the districts you genuinely serve where it reads naturally: in the page describing your service area, in an FAQ answering "do you work in my neighbourhood", in a case study that mentions where the client was. A footer listing twenty district names is the pattern search engines demote, and it reads as spam to a person too.

**One strong area page beats five thin ones.** Five near-identical district pages differing only in a swapped name are doorway pages, and a young domain has no authority to absorb the penalty. We built [one Beirut page](/web-design-beirut/) rather than five, for exactly that reason.

## Does a .lb domain help?

Not for ranking.

A `.com.lb` signals that you are a Lebanese business, which is worth something if all your customers are here. It is not a ranking advantage, and it carries three costs: registration requires documentation proving your registered business name, the process takes time, and it is harder to move if you ever sell abroad.

A `.com` is the right default for almost everyone. Plenty of businesses register both and point one at the other, which is cheap and removes the question.

What actually signals local relevance is the content, your business details being consistent everywhere, and links from other local sites. The domain extension is well down the list.

## What about hosting — does it need to be in Lebanon?

No, and it is usually the worse option.

A static site served from a global network reaches a visitor in Beirut from whichever edge location is nearest, which is faster than a single server here and unaffected by local outages. Your site stays up during a power cut because there is no machine here to lose power.

Local hosting mainly makes sense if a regulation or a client contract requires data to stay in the country. For an ordinary business site it adds cost, adds a single point of failure, and slows things down.

Server location is a weak ranking signal at best, and it is comprehensively outweighed by [the site being fast](/blog/building-for-slow-connections/), which matters more here than in markets with better connectivity.

## What does the competitive picture actually look like?

Better than in most markets, and this is the part worth acting on.

In a saturated market, technical work gets you parity — everyone has fast pages and structured data, so it buys you nothing and content decides. Here, a large share of competing sites are on heavy page builders, failing Core Web Vitals on mobile, with partial or absent structured data.

That means the ordinary technical baseline — [a fast site](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/), [clean structured data](/blog/structured-data-explained/), real answers to real questions — is doing something most competitors are not. The gap shows up faster than the same work would elsewhere.

The thing that does not change: **a new domain has no authority.** No amount of technical work substitutes for time and links. Expect months, not weeks, and treat anyone promising otherwise accordingly.

## What should a Lebanese business do first?

In order, because it maps effort to return.

1. **Check the searches exist.** If nobody searches for what you sell, ranking for it is worth nothing and your money belongs in ads or a better site. This is the step most often skipped.
2. **Fix the site.** Speed, mobile, structure. Everything downstream amplifies whatever the page already does, including failing.
3. **Name your areas honestly,** in the copy, where it reads naturally.
4. **Write the FAQ.** Your real customer questions, in the language they ask them in, answered in under 80 words.
5. **Get the details consistent.** Name, address, phone identical everywhere they appear. Inconsistency is the most common reason a business is understood as two different businesses.
6. **Then chase links.** Slowest, hardest, and what decides close calls.
