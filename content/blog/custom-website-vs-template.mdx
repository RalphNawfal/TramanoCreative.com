A template is the right choice more often than a studio selling custom work would like to admit. If you need a handful of pages, your customers find you through referral rather than search, and nothing about how you sell is unusual, buy the template and spend the difference on something else. Custom starts paying when speed, search visibility, or a specific flow through your site decides whether you get the enquiry.

## When is a template genuinely the right call?

Four situations, and they are common.

**You are testing whether the business works.** Spending $3,000 on a website for something you are not yet sure anyone wants is a bad use of money. Get something live, find out, then invest.

**Your customers do not arrive through search.** If every client comes from referral, Instagram, or a trade network, the technical search advantages of a custom build are worth very little to you. Buy the brochure.

**You genuinely need three pages.** Home, about, contact. There is not much for a designer to do that a good template has not already done.

**Your budget is under $500 and it has to stay there.** A cheap custom site is worse than a good template. We would rather tell you that than sell you a stripped-down build.

If two or more of those describe you, stop reading and go buy a template. We are not going to pretend otherwise.

## What breaks first on a template site?

Three things, roughly in this order.

**Speed.** This is the one that costs you money invisibly. Page builders generate the code to support every layout the platform offers, not the one you actually built, so a simple page arrives carrying several megabytes of scripts and styles. Add a dozen plugins and you have a dozen more. Load time is a ranking factor and a conversion factor simultaneously, so a slow site loses twice — [the full picture is here](/blog/website-speed-google-rankings/).

**The flow that does not fit.** Every template encodes assumptions about how a business sells. Most are built around a fairly generic path: read about us, browse services, fill in a form. The moment your actual sales process differs — a booking that has to happen in two taps, a menu that has to work one-handed at midnight, a quote request that needs four specific answers before it is useful — you are fighting the theme. You can usually force it. The result reads as forced.

**The redesign.** Templates age, and they age together. A theme that looked current in 2023 looks like 2023 now, and so do the thousands of other sites using it. Refreshing means either buying another template and migrating, or rebuilding — which is the cost you were avoiding, arriving late.

## What does "custom" actually mean, since everyone claims it?

The word is nearly meaningless in marketing copy, so ask two specific questions instead.

**"Was this design drawn for my business, or adapted from a theme?"** A design invented for you starts from what your customers need to decide and works backwards. An adapted theme starts from a layout that already exists and fits your content into it. Both produce a website. Only one is shaped by your business.

**"Is the code written for this site, or generated?"** Hand-written code contains what the page needs. Generated code contains what the generator supports. That difference is most of the speed gap, and it does not close with optimisation because it is architectural.

A studio doing genuinely custom work answers both without hedging. Vagueness — "we customise everything to your brand" — usually means a purchased theme with your colours applied.

## What does the difference actually cost?

Templates run from nothing to a few hundred dollars for the theme, plus your time or a few hundred more for someone to set it up. Custom work runs from $500 for a single focused page to $1,000–$3,000 for most full business sites. The [cost pillar breaks down what drives that number](/blog/how-much-does-a-custom-website-cost/).

The honest comparison is not the sticker price, though. It is:

- **Template:** low up front, ongoing platform fee, gets slower as plugins accumulate, and a rebuild in three to four years.
- **Custom:** higher up front, minimal hosting, stays fast because nothing accumulates, and it ages on your schedule rather than the theme's.

Over five years the totals are closer than they look at signing. Over one year, the template wins clearly. Which of those horizons you are actually planning for is the real question, and only you can answer it.

## Is a template bad for SEO?

No — and anyone telling you it is has oversimplified.

You can rank perfectly well on a template. Search engines do not care what generated the HTML, only what it contains and how fast it arrives. Plenty of template sites outrank plenty of custom ones, because they answer the question better.

What a template costs you is at the margins, and the margins matter in a competitive category:

- **Speed** is a genuine ranking factor and templates start behind.
- **Structured data** is possible on builders but usually plugin-dependent and partial.
- **Control over structure** — heading hierarchy, semantic markup, what loads when — is limited by what the platform exposes.

In an uncompetitive market, none of that decides anything. In a crowded one, where two sites answer a search equally well, it is exactly what separates them.

## How do you decide?

One question: **does your website have to win, or just exist?**

A site that has to exist — so people can check you are real, find your number, see your hours — should be a template. Spend the money on the thing that actually brings customers in.

A site that has to win — because customers compare you against three competitors before contacting anyone, because you are buying traffic and every click has to convert, because search is how people find businesses like yours — should be built properly. At that point the template is costing you more than it saves.

Most businesses know which one they are. The ones that get it wrong usually overspend on a site nobody was ever going to compare, or underspend on one that is their entire sales floor.
