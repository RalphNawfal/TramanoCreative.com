Ask five agencies what a website costs and you'll get five evasive answers. Here are real numbers instead.

## The short answer

In 2026, a professionally designed and developed custom website typically costs:

- **$500 – $1,000** — a focused landing page or micro-site: one to three pages, custom design, built for a single conversion goal.
- **$1,000 – $3,000** — a full business website: five to ten pages, custom design, blog, SEO foundation, contact and lead capture. This is where most projects land.
- **$3,000 – $8,000** — a flagship site: everything above plus advanced motion or 3D, content strategy, copywriting, and deeper conversion engineering.
- **$8,000+** — complex builds: e-commerce, membership areas, integrations, or large content migrations.

Those numbers are lower than most agency price lists, and the reason is structural rather than a discount: a two-person studio has no office, no account managers and no project managers to fund, so more of what you pay goes into the build itself.

You'll also see template work at the bottom of this range — a bought theme, skinned with your logo, for a few hundred dollars. That's fine for some businesses, but understand what you're buying: a look thousands of other sites share, on a stack that gets slower every year. The question worth asking at $500 isn't *how much* but *what's underneath it*.

## What actually drives the price

**Design depth.** A template skinned with your logo takes hours. A design invented for your brand — layout, motion, art direction — takes weeks. This is the single biggest cost lever.

**Page count and content.** Every page needs design, copy, and build time. Writing your own copy saves money; professional copy usually pays for itself in conversions.

**Interactivity.** Animations, 3D scenes, and cursor-reactive experiences (like the one on this site) add engineering time. They also make you memorable — which is the point.

**SEO engineering.** Anyone can install an SEO plugin. Structured data on every page, sub-second load times, and content architecture around real search demand is actual work, and it's where the long-term traffic comes from. It's also why [site speed is worth paying for](/blog/website-speed-google-rankings/) rather than treating as a nice-to-have.

## What you should refuse to pay for

- **Monthly "licensing" of your own website.** You should own your code, content, and domain outright.
- **Page-builder markup taxes.** Sites built on heavyweight builders load slower, and speed is a ranking factor. You'll pay again later in lost traffic.
- **Vague "maintenance" without deliverables.** A good care plan lists exactly what you get — updates, monitoring, content changes, reporting.

## How to budget

Work backwards from what a customer is worth. If your average client brings in $1,000, a $2,000 site needs to earn its cost back twice over the years it will run — which is a low bar for something that works every hour of every day. A website isn't a cost center — a *bad* website is.
