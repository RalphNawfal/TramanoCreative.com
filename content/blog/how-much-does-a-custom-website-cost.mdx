Ask five agencies what a website costs and you'll get five evasive answers. Here are real numbers instead: most custom business websites cost between $1,000 and $3,000, focused single-page builds start around $500, and anything past $8,000 is buying complexity rather than quality. What follows is where those numbers come from, what moves them, and what you should refuse to pay for.

## What does a custom website cost in 2026?

In 2026, a professionally designed and developed custom website typically costs:

- **$500 – $1,000** — a focused landing page or micro-site: one to three pages, custom design, built for a single conversion goal.
- **$1,000 – $3,000** — a full business website: five to ten pages, custom design, blog, SEO foundation, contact and lead capture. This is where most projects land.
- **$3,000 – $8,000** — a flagship site: everything above plus advanced motion or 3D, content strategy, copywriting, and deeper conversion engineering.
- **$8,000+** — complex builds: e-commerce, membership areas, integrations, or large content migrations.

Those numbers are lower than most agency price lists, and the reason is structural rather than a discount: a two-person studio has no office, no account managers and no project managers to fund, so more of what you pay goes into the build itself.

You'll also see template work at the bottom of this range — a bought theme, skinned with your logo, for a few hundred dollars. That's fine for some businesses, but understand what you're buying: a look thousands of other sites share, on a stack that gets slower every year. The question worth asking at $500 isn't *how much* but *what's underneath it*.

## Why do quotes for the same website vary by five times?

You will get quotes of $800 and $6,000 for a brief you described identically. Both can be honest. The gap is mostly overheads and scope, and it's worth knowing which one you're looking at.

**Overheads are the bigger half.** An agency with an office, a receptionist, account managers, project managers and a sales team carries real payroll, and it lands in every quote whether or not any of those people touch your project. That isn't dishonest — it's arithmetic. But it means a large share of a big quote is buying you a structure, not design or code. The useful question to ask any agency is what proportion of the fee reaches the people actually building the thing.

**Scope is the rest.** "A website" covers a bought theme with your logo dropped in and a design invented from scratch for your business, and both get sold under the same word. One is a few hours of configuration. The other is weeks of design, writing, building and testing. If two quotes are five times apart, ask each one how many pages, how much of the copy they're writing, and whether the design is original or adapted. The answers usually explain the whole gap.

**Where a small studio genuinely lands cheaper.** No lease, no account managers, nobody on commission. That's the entire discount, and it's structural rather than generous. The trade-off is real and worth stating: a two-person studio takes few projects at a time, so it isn't always available, and if you need a department of twelve, it's the wrong choice.

## What actually drives the number up or down?

**Design depth.** A template skinned with your logo takes hours. A design invented for your brand — layout, motion, art direction — takes weeks. This is the single biggest cost lever, and it's the one most people underestimate, because a finished design looks inevitable and gives no clue how many discarded versions preceded it.

**Page count and content.** Every page needs design, copy and build time. Writing your own copy saves real money. Getting it written for you usually pays for itself, because the page that converts is the one that answers the question a visitor arrived with, and most business owners are too close to their own business to hear that question clearly.

**Interactivity.** Animation and cursor-reactive detail add engineering time. Used well they make a site memorable, which is the point. Used badly they add weight to a page and slow it down, which costs you more than the effect is worth.

**SEO engineering.** Anyone can install an SEO plugin. Structured data on every page, sub-second load times and content built around real search demand is actual work, and it's where the long-term traffic comes from. It's also why [site speed is worth paying for](/blog/website-speed-google-rankings/) rather than treating as a nice-to-have.

**Integrations.** Booking systems, payment processing, inventory, a CRM. Each one is a moving part that has to be built, tested and maintained. This is the usual reason a project crosses $8,000, and it's worth being ruthless about which ones you actually need on day one.

## What does $500 actually buy?

One page, done properly. Custom design, copy that works, sub-second loading, and one clear thing you want a visitor to do.

It does not buy multiple pages, a blog, a content system you log into, or ongoing search work. If a template would do instead, [that comparison is here](/blog/custom-website-vs-template/). If you need those, you're in the $1,000–$3,000 range, and it's better to hear that before you commit than to buy the cheap option and buy it again in six months.

The case for spending $500 rather than $2,000 is genuinely strong in two situations: you're testing whether a market exists before investing in it, or you have one specific thing to sell and one specific action you want. In both, a single excellent page beats a mediocre eight-page site.

## What are the costs nobody puts in the quote?

The build is a one-off. Running the site is not, and this is where budgets get caught out.

- **Hosting.** For a static site, often under $20 a month and sometimes nothing. For a heavy platform with a database, more. Ask before you sign which one you're getting.
- **The domain.** $10–$30 a year. Trivial, but make sure it's registered in your name, not the agency's.
- **Content changes.** Some studios include a number of changes; some bill each one. Ask what a small text edit costs and how fast it happens.
- **Copywriting.** Frequently excluded from a quote and then discovered to be the thing blocking launch. If you're not writing it, someone is charging for it.
- **Stock images and fonts.** Should be billed at cost. If they're marked up, ask why.
- **The rebuild you'll need in three years** if it's built on a platform that ages badly. This is the expensive one, and it never appears in any quote.

## Should you pay a fixed price or by the hour?

Fixed price, agreed before work starts, in a currency that holds its value.

Hourly billing puts every estimating mistake on you. If the project takes twice as long as expected, you pay twice as much — including for the time spent solving problems the studio created. It also removes any incentive to work efficiently, which is a strange thing to build into an agreement.

A studio that won't quote a fixed price usually doesn't understand the scope well enough to price it. That's useful information, and [there's more on why the structure matters more than the number](/blog/fixed-price-vs-hourly-web-design/). The reasonable middle ground on a large project is fixed milestones: a defined price for a defined stage, so neither side is exposed to the whole project at once.

## How should you budget for it?

Work backwards from what a customer is worth.

If your average client brings in $1,000, a $2,000 site needs to earn its cost back twice across the years it will run. That's a low bar for something that works every hour of every day, in every time zone, without being paid. If your average client is worth $50, the maths is different and a $500 page is probably the right call — and any studio worth hiring will tell you that rather than selling you the bigger option.

A website isn't a cost centre. A *bad* website is, and so is an expensive one bought for a business that wasn't ready for it.

## What's different about buying a website in Lebanon?

Three things, and they change the decision more than design taste does.

**Quote in dollars, fixed.** Work that takes weeks cannot honestly be priced in a currency that may move during those weeks. A fixed number in USD, agreed at the start, is the only version where the price you agree is the price you pay.

**Payments are the hard part, not the site.** Getting a small business approved for online card payments here is slow and often doesn't happen at all. Establish what you can actually get approved for before anyone designs a checkout. There's more on this in [web design in Lebanon](/web-design-lebanon/) and in [our Beirut case study](/work/burger-shop/), where the answer was a WhatsApp handoff rather than a card form.

**Build light, because connections vary.** A visitor on mobile data in Beirut is not on office fibre in Dubai. A heavy site loses those people before it finishes loading, and they don't come back.

## How long should it take, and does faster cost more?

Three to six weeks from kickoff for a typical business site. A single landing page ships in under two weeks. Anything past two months usually means either a genuinely complex build or a studio running your project alongside too many others.

Speed and price aren't as linked as people expect. A studio that controls its whole stack moves fast because there's nothing to wait on — no plugin compatibility to test, no third party to chase. The projects that take longest are rarely the most technical ones. They're the ones where the content isn't written and everyone is waiting on a paragraph.

That's worth planning for. Content is the single most common reason a launch date slips, and it slips on the client's side far more often than the studio's. If you know you won't have time to write it, get that into the quote at the start rather than discovering it in week four.

Beware anyone promising a full custom site in a week. Either it's a template, or the parts that don't show — speed, structure, search — are being skipped.

## What should you ask before you hire anyone?

Five questions. The answers tell you more than any portfolio.

**"Who owns the code, the domain and the accounts?"** The answer should be you, without qualification. If there's hesitation, or the domain sits in the agency's name, walk away.

**"Is this a fixed price or an estimate?"** If it's an estimate, ask what happens when it's exceeded and who pays.

**"Who is actually doing the work?"** Sometimes the person selling is the person building. Often they aren't. Neither is wrong, but you should know which before you sign, because a hand-off is where understanding gets lost.

**"What happens if I want to leave?"** Ask it plainly. The good answer is that you take everything and nothing breaks. Any other answer is a cost that doesn't appear in the quote.

**"What's the launch date, and what happens if it moves?"** A studio that won't commit to a date usually can't predict its own capacity.

## What should you refuse to pay for?

- **Monthly "licensing" of your own website.** You should own the code, the content and the domain outright. Anything else means leaving costs you the site.
- **Page-builder markup taxes.** Sites built on heavyweight builders load slower, and speed is a ranking factor. You pay again later, in traffic you never see.
- **Vague "maintenance" without deliverables.** A real care plan lists what you get: updates, monitoring, content changes, reporting, and how fast each happens.
- **A discovery phase billed separately** before anyone will give you a number. Understanding your business is how a studio earns the work, not a product.
- **Anything you can't take with you.** Ask what happens if you leave. A surprising amount of agency work is built so that leaving means starting over, and that's a price you pay later without ever seeing it quoted.

## How do you get a real number for your project?

Describe the business, not the website. How you get customers today, what you want to happen when someone finds you, and what you've already tried. A studio that understands those three things can price the work in one conversation; one that can't is going to guess and then bill you for the guess.
