Most diners in Lebanon find a restaurant on Google Maps or Instagram, check the menu and the hours, and then order on a delivery app or by WhatsApp. To win that sequence, a restaurant needs three things working together: a Google Maps listing that's complete and correct, a menu written as text that Google can read, and a direct way to order that doesn't hand a commission to a delivery app. Our [restaurant website](/restaurant-website-design-lebanon/) page covers how we build the site part.

## Why does Google Maps matter more than the website?

Because it's usually the first thing a hungry person sees. Someone searching "burger near me" or "brunch Mar Mikhael" gets a map with a handful of places, each showing a rating, photos, hours and whether it's open now. Many decide right there.

So the listing has to be right before the website matters at all:

- **The pin in the exact spot.** In streets without clear numbering, a pin on the wrong block sends people to a closed shutter.
- **The right category,** so you appear for the searches you want.
- **Hours that are true today,** including holidays and Ramadan hours.
- **Phone and WhatsApp,** so people can ask or order from the listing.
- **Recent photos** of the food and the space.
- **A link to your menu page and website.**

The listing is a Google Business Profile, and it's free. If you don't have one, it's the first thing to set up. [Local SEO in Lebanon](/blog/local-seo-lebanon/) covers it in more detail.

## Why shouldn't the menu be a photo or a PDF?

Because nobody can read it except a person squinting at a phone, slowly.

A menu posted as a photo or a PDF:

- **Can't be found by searching a dish.** Google can't reliably read the text inside an image.
- **Can't be quoted by AI assistants.** When someone asks ChatGPT "where can I get good shawarma in Achrafieh", only menus written as text can be part of the answer.
- **Is heavy on mobile data,** and often unreadable without zooming.
- **Goes out of date quietly.** Changing one price means redesigning the image.

A menu written as text on a web page fixes all four. Each item has a name, a description and a price; it loads instantly; it can be updated in a minute; and it's readable by people, search engines and assistants alike. If your customers read in both Arabic and English, put both in the same item rather than making them choose a language first.

## Delivery apps or direct orders?

Both, for different customers.

**Delivery apps** bring you people who have never heard of you, and handle the drivers. In exchange they charge a commission on every order, including orders from your regulars who would have ordered from you anyway.

**Direct orders** through your own site are for the people who already know you: regulars, people who saw your Instagram, people who searched your name. Every one of those orders that moves from an app to your own site keeps the commission.

The direct route doesn't need an app or a card processor. A menu with a cart that sends the finished order to you on WhatsApp is enough. We built one for a burger shop in Beirut that had no website before: in its first eleven weeks it took [61 orders worth $1,129](/work/burger-shop/) through the site, with no ad spend. When the shop started posting regularly on Instagram, the site took 25 orders in 16 days, more than in either of its first two months.

## What should the ordering flow do?

Make the order exact, so nothing has to be clarified on the phone:

- **Items, options and quantities** chosen on the menu, with a running total.
- **Name, phone and area,** so you know where it's going and what delivery costs.
- **Pickup or delivery,** and any notes.
- **One complete message** sent to your WhatsApp, ready to confirm.

Then tell the customer what happens next: you'll confirm within a few minutes, and they pay on delivery. [WhatsApp and your website](/blog/whatsapp-and-your-website/) goes into how to build that message.

## How do you keep hours and menus from going stale?

Restaurants change more often than almost any other business: new dishes, price changes, holiday hours, a kitchen closed for a week. The rule that saves the most customers is simple. Change the website, the Google listing and the Instagram bio at the same time, every time.

Whoever updates them needs to be able to do it without calling a developer. A menu that only the agency can edit is a menu that will be wrong by next month.

## What about reviews?

Reply to them, good and bad, on Google. Replies show people reading the listing that someone is paying attention, and a calm reply to a bad review often does more for your reputation than the review did harm. Ask happy regulars to leave one; most people will if asked.
